Political Theory · Gender & Sexuality · Social & Political Philosophy

Language, disagreement, friendship, and political life.

I am a political theorist and teacher working in the tradition of ordinary language philosophy. My research asks how shared concepts make political disagreement, conversation, and forms of closeness possible — and how those same concepts can obstruct them.

Teaching

Four writing-intensive courses designed and taught at Johns Hopkins, with additional teaching in classical and modern political thought, international relations theory, and U.S. foreign policy.

Teaching

Political theory as a practice of reading, argument, and conversation.

My courses are writing-intensive and discussion-centered. I ask students to reconstruct difficult arguments, identify assumptions, test rival interpretations, and use disagreement as a way of becoming more precise about what they mean.

Research

Ordinary language philosophy and the politics of concepts.

My dissertation, On Saying What You Want to Say: A Wittgensteinian Approach, develops an ordinary-language approach to conceptual disagreement. My current work extends that project into political disagreement and the philosophy of friendship.

About

I received my Ph.D. in Political Science (Political Theory) from Johns Hopkins University in 2023, where my dissertation committee included Jennifer Culbert, Michael Williams, Jane Bennett, Samuel Chambers, and Rochelle Tobias. I then served as a postdoctoral fellow in the Alexander Grass Humanities Institute's Society of Fellows and the Program for the Study of Women, Gender, and Sexuality.

I am based in Brooklyn. Alongside academic work, I have worked in public-facing journalism and in AI evaluation, where I continue to think about language, judgment, and the practical consequences of conceptual categories.
